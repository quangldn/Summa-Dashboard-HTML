The thesis in one line
Two forces — Singapore's cable-landing saturation and its data-center power moratorium — push both compute and connectivity to Johor and Batam. That single displacement creates the densest new DCI corridor in Asia: new cable landings + terrestrial backhaul from cable landing stations (CLS) to inland AI campuses. The DC report and this subsea report describe the same corridor from two ends.

What's happening in the water (2024–2028)

  • An AI-driven cable wave. Bifrost, Echo and Apricot (Google/Meta) went live 2024–25; ADC and SJC2 lit Vietnam/SE Asia 2024–25; SEA-ME-WE 6, ALC and SEA-H2X land 2025–26; Meta's Candle (570 Tbps, 24 fibre-pairs) arrives 2028 as the largest-capacity APAC system. Fibre-pair counts are climbing because AI training clusters need east–west bandwidth on a new scale.
  • Routes are bending around geopolitics. Bifrost and Echo are the first trans-Pacific systems routed via the Java/Celebes Sea to avoid the South China Sea; SJC2 slipped >1 year on China territorial-water permitting near Hong Kong. Hong Kong is fading as the pivot; the Philippines (Luzon), Japan and Guam are rising.
  • Singapore is full — so the region spreads. Landing sites are first-come-first-served with no new major sites; overflow lands in Johor (Malaysia) and Batam (Indonesia) — exactly where hyperscale DC capacity is going.

Diagnostic — resilience, chokepoints, geopolitics

A subsea network is only as good as its diversity. Where the ASEAN wet plant is fragile, and why.

Chokepoints & physical risk

  • Luzon Strait — the trans-Pacific / Hong Kong approach funnels dozens of cables through a narrow, seismically active corridor. A single event can degrade Asia–US capacity broadly.
  • Malacca & Singapore Strait — 80,000–90,000 vessels/year; anchor-drag and fishing damage are the leading fault cause. Everything into the Singapore hub crosses it.
  • South China Sea — ~⅓ of global shipping and contested waters; repair permitting is slow. New cables (Bifrost, Echo) deliberately route around it via the Java/Celebes Sea.

Geopolitical & regulatory friction

  • China territorial-water permitting — delayed SJC2 by >1 year around Hong Kong; a structural reason Hong Kong is losing hub share to Japan/Philippines/Singapore.
  • Indonesia cabotage law — cable work requires Indonesian-flagged vessels, slowing installation and repair on any system touching Indonesian waters (i.e., most trans-Pacific routes now).
  • Undelimited maritime boundaries (SG–MY–ID) limit independent route management and add negotiation drag.

Diagnostic takeaway

The ASEAN wet plant is simultaneously booming and brittle. Capacity is being added fast, but it concentrates on a few chokepoints and one saturated hub. Every resilience move — a new Vietnam-owned cable, a Batam/Johor overflow landing, a South-China-Sea-avoiding route — is a new terrestrial backhaul and DCI requirement. Fragility is the demand signal.

The corridor map (say this to Sales)
The three DCI corridors that both reports point to: Johor ↔ Singapore (DC overflow + cable overflow, cross-strait), Batam ↔ Singapore (Indonesia's cross-border twin, trans-Pacific landings), and HCMC ↔ Bình Dương + Vietnam CLS (Quy Nhơn/Đà Nẵng/Vũng Tàu landings back to the southern DC cluster). These are where new cable landings and new DC capacity coincide — the highest-probability terrestrial optical demand in ASEAN.

Why connecting the two reports matters

  • A DC announcement is a leading indicator of a subsea landing + backhaul, and vice versa. Track them together and you see the optical demand before the RFP.
  • The near-term, defensible optics revenue is terrestrial — CLS-to-DC backhaul and metro DCI — not the wet plant. The subsea wave is the reason the terrestrial demand exists.
  • In Vietnam the cable owners and the DC builders are the same accounts (Viettel/VNPT/FPT/CMC) — one relationship covers both the landing and the backhaul.

Nokia / Optics Play in Subsea

An honest read of where Nokia can and can't sell in the subsea value chain — post the Alcatel Submarine Networks divestiture, and post the Infinera acquisition.

Reality check: Nokia completed the sale of Alcatel Submarine Networks (ASN) — the wet-plant / cable-and-repeater manufacturing business — to the French State on 3 January 2025. Nokia no longer builds submarine cables or repeaters. Don't pitch the wet plant. The Nokia subsea story is now the dry side and the terrestrial side — and, thanks to Infinera, that story is stronger, not weaker.

The positioning wedge

  • Infinera changes the subsea conversation. Infinera's ICE6 has a real track record as open-cable SLTE on trans-oceanic systems; folded into Nokia, it gives a credible dry-side story that pre-acquisition Nokia lacked. Lead with capability, not specific named wins you can't verify.
  • Sell the terrestrial join, not the wet plant. Every new landing (and there are many) needs SLTE + backhaul. That's the addressable, winnable scope.
  • Efficiency into the power gate. Same argument as the DC report: high-baud coherent → fewer regens → lower W/bit, which matters at power-constrained landing/DC sites.

What this dashboard is built on, and how confident to be in each part.

Primary sources

  • Carnegie Endowment — Subsea Communication Cables in Southeast Asia — regional resilience / permitting risk.
  • Meta & Google engineering blogs — Candle (570 Tbps, 24 FP, 2028), Bifrost, Echo, Apricot routes/capacity.

  • Nokia newsroom — completion of the ASN sale to the French State (3 Jan 2025).

Confidence & caveats

  • Cable counts vary by definition (in-service vs including planned; international vs domestic). Singapore "~28" (CSIS) vs an older "17 active" (2016) reflects growth, not error. Vietnam "5–6" counts in-service international systems; add SJC2/VTS and it's higher.
  • Capacity figures are design/potential, not lit capacity — treat as ceilings.
  • RFS dates for planned cables slip routinely (SJC2 slipped >1 yr). Planned = intent, not schedule.
  • The Nokia/Infinera SLTE positioning is a capability statement — verify specific cable wins against current Nokia references before quoting one to a customer.
  • For authoritative, current detail on any single cable, drop back to TeleGeography submarinecablemap.com.
